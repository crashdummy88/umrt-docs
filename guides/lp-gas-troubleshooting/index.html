Field notes

LP gas problems are safety-sensitive by nature. Here’s how to tell a routine fix from something that needs a professional immediately — plus the regulator, tank, and detector reference every owner should know. If you smell gas, hear hissing, or your LP or CO detector alarms, shut off the supply valve at the tank, evacuate, and do not operate any switches, lighters, or appliances until the system has been checked by a professional.

Appliances Won’t Light

— Empty or near-empty tank — check the gauge first, it’s the most common cause — Tank valve not fully open, or a closed shutoff valve somewhere in the line — Regulator has tripped into bypass mode after a rapid pressu.

Low Flame, Yellow Flame, or Soot

A healthy propane flame burns blue. Yellow or orange flame, visible soot, or a weak flame all point to a combustion or pressure problem — not something to ignore.

LP Detector Alarming with No Obvious Cause

— Detectors have a service life of roughly 5 years from date of manufacture — check the manufacture date before assuming a leak; the internal sensor degrades with age regardless of use — Cleaning products, aerosols, a.

Regulators & Auto-Changeover Valves

Most RVs use a two-stage regulator: a first stage that drops tank pressure to roughly 10 PSI, then a second stage that drops it to the appliance-ready 11″ W.C. (about 0.5 PSI).

Cylinders vs. ASME Tanks

— DOT cylinders (portable, vertical, removable — typical on trailers) require recertification every 12 years from manufacture, stamped on the collar — ASME tanks (permanently mounted, horizontal — typical on motorhome.

When to Stop and Call a Professional

Any smell of gas, a soap-bubble test that shows a leak, a regulator that won’t reset, or repeated detector alarms all warrant a full leak-down test and system inspection.
