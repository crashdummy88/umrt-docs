RV Solar Panel Sizing & Installation Guide

Most solar installs fail the owner not because the panels are bad, but because the system was sized to the roof instead of the actual daily power draw. Here’s how to size it correctly before you buy anything.

Source: WP Field Guide. Corridor: Montana · Wyoming · Idaho · Washington — case-by-case beyond.

Field notes

Start with daily watt-hours, not panel wattage

Add up the watt-hours every 12V device draws per day. The live guide’s examples:

  • Compressor fridge — 600–900 Wh
  • CPAP — 30–60 Wh
  • Lighting — 100–200 Wh
  • Water pump and fans — 100–150 Wh
  • Plus anything run through the inverter

A boondocking-focused rig commonly lands between 1,500 and 3,000 Wh/day. That number, not roof space, is what sizes the system.

Rule of thumb (from WP). In useful sun (4–5 peak-sun hours average across seasons), you need roughly 1 watt of panel for every 4–5 Wh of daily draw to keep pace, plus headroom for cloudy days and charge losses. 2,000 Wh/day realistically wants 400–600 W of panel.

Battery bank comes second

Panels charge the bank; the bank is what actually gets you through the night and cloudy stretches. Size usable capacity to 1.5–2 days of draw with no sun at all. With lithium (100% usable) that’s simpler math than with AGM (50% usable). Undersized panels with an oversized bank just means slow recovery. Oversized panels with an undersized bank means wasted capacity on clear days. They have to be sized together.

Work the same starting numbers on the WP off-grid power budget guide (list every device, add a 20–30% buffer for inverter/wiring/charge losses, then size battery, solar, and inverter together).

What installation actually involves

Roof mount & seal

A bad panel seal is a future roof leak, not a solar problem.

Wire routing

Sized for the run length to avoid voltage drop.

Charge controller

Matched to panel voltage and battery chemistry. See MPPT vs PWM.

Fusing

Correct fusing/breakers at every stage from panel to controller to battery. Skipping the fusing is the most common corner cut — and the most dangerous one.

DIY vs book a visit

Reasonable DIY

Adding up daily watt-hours and reading the bank math before you order panels.
